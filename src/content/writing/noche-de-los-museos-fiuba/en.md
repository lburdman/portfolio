On Saturday 8 November 2025, from 19:00, the Facultad de Ingeniería opened its doors at Av. Las Heras 2214 for La Noche de los Museos. The faculty was invited because of the interest the Qiskit Fall Fest FIUBA had generated, and the invitation arrived in the middle of that festival's month.

The framing was the International Year of Quantum Science and Technology: 2025 marks one hundred years of quantum mechanics. So the exhibition was built as a chronological walk through the milestones of the field, from the century's opening arguments to where the subject stands now. At the end of that walk there was a machine.

## The only two-qubit computer in the country

The special exhibition was the only two-qubit quantum computer in Argentina, based on nuclear magnetic resonance. It is a desktop unit. It sits on a table, with a lid that opens and a tablet attached to it, and that ordinariness is doing a lot of work for a visitor who has only ever seen quantum computing as a photograph of a gold chandelier in a laboratory.

Nuclear magnetic resonance is the same physics as a hospital MRI scanner. Certain atomic nuclei behave like tiny magnets: placed in a strong magnetic field, a nucleus has two distinguishable orientations with respect to that field, and those two orientations are the two states of a qubit. The qubits are therefore not fabricated. They are nuclei belonging to a molecule, and the molecule is chosen so that its nuclei sit at slightly different frequencies and can be addressed one at a time.

Addressing them means sending a radio-frequency pulse. A pulse of the right frequency and duration rotates a nuclear spin by a controlled amount, and a controlled rotation is exactly what a quantum gate is. Neighbouring nuclei in the molecule feel each other through their chemical bonds, and that coupling is what makes a two-qubit gate possible. Read-out is the faint signal the spins induce in a coil as they precess.

Two qubits is not a computer that will factor anything or beat a laptop at any task, and it is important to say so plainly. It is a teaching instrument. That is precisely the point of putting it in a room full of people.

## What the screen showed

The tablet displayed a two-qubit circuit: a Hadamard gate on the first qubit, then a CNOT onto the second. Two gates, to look at, and the most famous short program in the field: it prepares a Bell pair, the simplest entangled state there is.

Below the circuit was a chart labelled Projection Probability, and it drew two sets of bars: Real Result against Simulate Result. The simulated bars are what the mathematics says should happen. The real bars are what the nuclear spins in the molecule actually did.

They did not match perfectly.

That gap is the whole lesson, and it is worth walking across a city to see it rendered on a desk rather than described on a slide. Real qubits lose their quantum state to their environment while you are still working with them, and every pulse you send is very slightly the wrong pulse. Decoherence and gate error stop being terms in a glossary the moment you can point at the two bars that disagree. Everything difficult about building a quantum computer — error correction, longer coherence times, better control — is an attempt to close a gap of exactly that kind.

The Qiskit Fall Fest talks and activities continued through the rest of November.
